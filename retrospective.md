
# Retrospective — AI Fluency Track

## Looking Back from Week 8

When I started this track in Week 1, my main goal was to become more confident at building and shipping real projects instead of only learning concepts or completing isolated tasks. I wanted to understand how AI could fit into my existing software engineering skills and help me move faster without becoming dependent on it. At the beginning, much of my work was focused on planning, portfolio structure, prompting, and understanding how to approach a project. By Week 8, I had worked across frontend development, backend APIs, databases, authentication, AI APIs, automation, agents, background jobs, documentation, and deployment.

One of the biggest changes was in how I approach building. Early in the track, I spent more time deciding what something should look like and how I should structure it. The portfolio assignments pushed me to make decisions about content, identity, visuals, CTAs, technology choices, and responsive behavior. Later, the backend assignments made me think more about how an application actually works behind the interface. I built a CRUD API, connected it to a database, containerized it, added authentication, worked with scraping, integrated an AI API, and then moved into workflows and background jobs. This helped me connect the frontend experience with the systems working behind it.

The agent work was another major shift. Designing my personal agent and then actually building the Internship & Learning Progress Coach made AI feel less like a tool that simply answers questions and more like something that can be designed around a specific workflow. I had to think about what information the agent needed, what decision it should make, how it should use project data, and how I could evaluate whether its output was actually useful. That changed the way I think about AI systems: the important part is not just the model, but the workflow, inputs, constraints, evaluation, and human judgment around it.

My approach to AI also changed. I learned that generating something with AI is only the beginning. Throughout the track I used AI for brainstorming, code generation, debugging, documentation, iteration, and feedback, but I also had to run the code, test the behavior, inspect errors, make decisions, and adapt the generated output. This made me more comfortable treating AI as a development partner rather than an authority whose first answer should automatically be accepted.

If I continued building after this track, I would take the strongest parts of my work and combine them into a more polished end-to-end product. I would improve the AI workflow system with stronger evaluation, better error handling, clearer user feedback, and more production-oriented deployment. I would also continue improving my personal site so that it becomes not just a portfolio but a clear demonstration of the projects, decisions, and engineering process behind my work.

### Three Transferable Lessons

**1. Build and test instead of waiting for perfect knowledge.**
I learned more by actually running APIs, fixing errors, deploying projects, and testing workflows than I would have by only studying the concepts first.

**2. AI output needs human verification.**
AI can accelerate development, but it does not remove the need to understand what was produced. Testing, debugging, reviewing, and making the final decision remain my responsibility.

**3. A good project is more than working code.**
Documentation, usability, evaluation, limitations, deployment, and the ability to explain design decisions are all part of building something that another person can trust and understand.

Looking back, the biggest change is not that I learned a list of new tools. It is that I became more comfortable moving from an idea to a working system, testing it, explaining it, and improving it. That is the mindset I want to carry into my next projects and internship work.
